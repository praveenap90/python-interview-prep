Note on performance: tuples are slightly faster to create and iterate and use a little less memory than lists, but the gap is small, so choose by mutability and intent, not speed. Sets and dicts use extra memory for their hash tables; that is the price of O(1) average membership and key lookups.

🔗 Set Operations: union, intersection, difference, symmetric_difference

Using a = {1,2,3} and b = {2,3,4} throughout.

OperationMethodOperatorReturnsModifies original?Result
Uniona.union(b)a | bElements in either setNo — new set{1,2,3,4}
Intersectiona.intersection(b)a & bElements in both setsNo — new set{2,3}
Intersection updatea.intersection_update(b)a &= bElements in both, rewrites a in place✅ Yesa becomes {2,3}
Differencea.difference(b)a - bElements in a but not bNo — new set{1}
Symmetric differencea.symmetric_difference(b)a ^ bElements in exactly one of the two setsNo — new set{1,4}
Everything except intersection_update() (and its siblings update(), difference_update(), symmetric_difference_update()) returns a brand-new set and leaves the originals untouched — the _update variants are the in-place versions, mutating the left-hand set instead of returning a new one.

🎤 Data Cleaning — Interview Answer Cheat Sheet

Interview questionStrong answer
How do you handle missing values?Understand why the data is missing, then drop, impute, or create a missing indicator depending on the business context — not a one-size-fits-all rule.
Mean vs. median?Median is preferred when data contains outliers or is skewed — it stays representative where the mean gets pulled off.
How do you handle duplicates?Identify duplicate keys, investigate the root cause, then remove or aggregate appropriately — don't just drop_duplicates() blindly without understanding why they exist.
How do you clean large datasets?Use vectorized pandas operations, chunk processing, SQL-side filtering, or Spark/Dask when the data no longer fits comfortably in memory.
How do you avoid data leakage during cleaning?Learn transformations (fill values, scalers, encodings) only from training data, then apply — never refit — them to validation/test data.
How do you productionize cleaning?Turn ad hoc steps into reusable functions, add validation checks and logging, wrap it in a pipeline, and monitor it in production so silent data drift gets caught.

SETUP

Generate the dataset (or swap in your own CSV)

Everything below assumes these five DataFrames already exist in memory.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)   # seeded -- reproducible every run

n_customers, n_products, n_orders = 2_000, 300, 20_000

regions = pd.DataFrame({
    "region_id": range(1, 6),
    "region_name": ["North America", "Europe", "Asia", "South America", "Africa"]
})

customers = pd.DataFrame({
    "customer_id": range(1, n_customers + 1),
    "segment": rng.choice(["Consumer", "Corporate", "Home Office"], n_customers, p=[.55, .30, .15]),
    "region_id": rng.integers(1, 6, n_customers)
})

products = pd.DataFrame({
    "product_id": range(1, n_products + 1),
    "category": rng.choice(["Furniture", "Office Supplies", "Technology"], n_products),
    "unit_price": rng.gamma(2, 50, n_products).round(2)
})

order_dates = pd.date_range("2021-01-01", "2024-07-31", freq="D")
orders = pd.DataFrame({
    "order_id": range(1, n_orders + 1),
    "customer_id": rng.integers(1, n_customers + 1, n_orders),
    "order_date": rng.choice(order_dates, n_orders),
    "ship_mode": rng.choice(["Standard", "Second Class", "First Class", "Same Day"],
                             n_orders, p=[.60, .20, .15, .05])
})
orders["commit_date"] = orders["order_date"] + pd.to_timedelta(5, unit="D")
orders["ship_date"]   = orders["order_date"] + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(1, 10, n_orders), unit="D")

# 1-4 line items per order
rows = []
for oid in orders["order_id"]:
    for _ in range(rng.integers(1, 5)):
        pid = rng.integers(1, n_products + 1)
        rows.append((oid, pid, rng.integers(1, 10),
                     rng.choice([0, .05, .10, .15, .20], p=[.4, .25, .2, .1, .05])))
order_items = pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["order_id", "product_id", "quantity", "discount"])
order_items = order_items.merge(products[["product_id", "unit_price"]], on="product_id")
order_items["net_revenue"] = (order_items["quantity"] * order_items["unit_price"]
                               * (1 - order_items["discount"]))
Note: order_items is the pandas equivalent of a line-item fact table — one row per product per order, same grain as a SQL lineitem table. Keep this in mind: aggregating at the order level before merging to line items will double-count, exactly as in SQL.
▸ the situation

A wholesale distributor asks: "Is our growth healthy?"

You're the analyst at a mid-size distributor — the kind of business this synthetic dataset models: it sells furniture, office supplies, and technology to customers across five regions, shipping by several modes. The VP of Sales sends this before the quarterly review:

Revenue feels flat and I keep hearing delivery complaints. Are we too dependent on a few customers or regions, and is delivery getting worse? I need a recommendation, not a data dump.
Questions
1. Is revenue concentrated in a few regions or customers?
2. Is growth coming from new or returning customers?
3. Is delivery performance trending worse?
Metric definitions
Net revenue = quantity × unit price × (1 − discount), at line level.
Late = shipped after the committed date.
New customer = first order ever in that year.
Guardrails
Reconcile totals before trusting a merge.
Compare like-for-like periods only.
Report a null finding plainly.

The company, the email, and the stakeholder are fictional, and the data is the synthetic dataset generated above. The workflow is the point: it mirrors how a real request moves from a vague question to a defensible recommendation.
